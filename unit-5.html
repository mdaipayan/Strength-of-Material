Mechanics Fundamentals

Physical Meaning of Torsion & Torque

A member is said to be in torsion when it is subjected to moments (or couples) that act about its longitudinal centroidal axis. These twisting couples are known as torques or twisting moments ($T$).

Bending vs. Torsion

  • Bending Moment ($M$): Vector parallel to the cross-section plane. Produces longitudinal tension on one side and compression on the other.
  • Twisting Moment ($T$): Vector perpendicular to the cross-section plane (along member axis). Causes adjacent cross-sections to rotate relative to each other.

Right-Hand Screw Rule

Curl the fingers of your right hand along the rotational direction of the torque; your extended thumb points along the double-arrow vector. A torque vector pointing outwards from a cut section is considered positive.

Deformation Under Pure Torsion

A straight longitudinal surface fiber $AB$ deforms into a helix $AB'$ under twisting torque $T$. Cross-sections rotate about the axis by angle $\theta$.

Coulomb's Theory

Assumptions in the Theory of Pure Torsion

The classical elementary theory of torsion for circular shafts is attributed to Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (1784). It is based upon the following rigorous mechanical assumptions:

1 Material is Homogeneous, Isotropic & Linear-Elastic

The material has identical elastic properties in all directions and obeys Hooke's Law in shear ($\tau = G\gamma$). Stresses remain strictly below the proportional limit in shear.

2 Plane Cross-Sections Remain Plane and Perpendicular to the Axis (No Warping)

A transverse section of the circular shaft remains plane and circular after twisting. There is no warping (out-of-plane axial displacement). This is strictly true only for circular sections due to complete axisymmetry! Non-circular cross-sections (square, rectangular, I-beams) warp significantly and violate this assumption.

3 Radial Lines Remain Straight

Any straight radial line drawn from the center of the circular cross-section to the boundary remains straight and simply rotates through an angle $\theta$ relative to the opposite end.

4 Axial Spacing Remains Constant

Cross-sections do not move axially toward or away from one another. The length of the shaft $L$ remains completely unchanged ($\epsilon_x = 0$).

5 Uniform Angle of Twist Along Length

The rate of twist $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{\theta}{L}$ is uniform and constant across the entire prismatic span of the shaft.

Crucial Exam Insight: Why can't we use Coulomb's formula for a rectangular bar? Because a rectangular cross-section warps into a saddle shape under torsion. The corners carry zero shear stress, and the maximum shear stress occurs at the middle of the longer sides (Saint-Venant's theory of torsion).

Mathematical Derivation

Derivation of the Fundamental Torsion Formula

The relationship connecting applied torque $T$, polar moment of inertia $J$, shear stress $\tau$, radius $r$, modulus of rigidity $G$, angle of twist $\theta$, and length $L$ is derived in three transparent stages:

Stage 1: Geometric Kinematics & Shear Strain

Consider a circular shaft of radius $R$ and length $L$ subjected to torque $T$. An element on the surface at distance $L$ from the fixed end rotates through angle $\theta$. The arc length traversed by point $B$ to $B'$ is:

$$\text{Arc length } BB' = R \cdot \theta$$

By definition, shear strain $\gamma$ is the change in the initial right angle between generator $AB$ and the end face:

$$\gamma_{surface} = \tan \angle BAB' \approx \frac{BB'}{L} = \frac{R\theta}{L}$$ $$\text{At any arbitrary radial distance } r: \quad \gamma(r) = \frac{r\theta}{L}$$

Key deduction: Shear strain varies linearly with radius from zero at the center to maximum at the surface!

Stage 2: Constitutive Stress-Strain Relation

Applying Hooke's Law in pure shear, where $G$ is the Modulus of Rigidity (Shear Modulus):

$$\tau(r) = G \cdot \gamma(r) = G \left(\frac{r\theta}{L}\right)$$ $$\implies \frac{\tau(r)}{r} = \frac{G\theta}{L}$$

Since $G$, $\theta$, and $L$ are constant for any given section:

$$\frac{\tau(r)}{r} = \frac{\tau_{max}}{R} = \text{constant}$$

Stage 3: Static Equilibrium with Internal Resisting Torque

Consider an elemental concentric ring of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$. Elemental area $dA = 2\pi r \, dr$.

$$\text{Elemental shear force } dF = \tau(r) \, dA = \left(\frac{\tau_{max}}{R} r\right) dA$$ $$\text{Elemental resisting torque } dT = dF \cdot r = \left(\frac{\tau_{max}}{R} r^2\right) dA$$ $$\text{Total torque } T = \int dT = \frac{\tau_{max}}{R} \int r^2 dA$$

By definition, the integral $\int r^2 dA$ represents the Polar Moment of Inertia ($J$) of the cross-section about the polar axis:

$$T = \frac{\tau_{max}}{R} \cdot J \implies \frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau_{max}}{R} = \frac{\tau}{r}$$

The Universal Torsion Equation

$$\frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{G\theta}{L}$$

Analogy with Pure Bending: $\dfrac{M}{I} = \dfrac{\sigma}{y} = \dfrac{E}{R}$

Section Properties

Polar Moment of Inertia ($J$) & Polar Section Modulus ($Z_p$)

By the Perpendicular Axis Theorem, for any plane area $J = I_{zz} = I_{xx} + I_{yy}$. For circular sections where $I_{xx} = I_{yy} = \frac{\pi D^4}{64}$:

Solid Circular Shaft

Integrating elemental rings $dA = 2\pi r dr$ from $r=0$ to $R = D/2$:

$$J = \int_0^{D/2} 2\pi r^3 dr = \frac{\pi D^4}{32}$$

Polar Section Modulus ($Z_p = J/R$):

$$Z_p = \frac{\frac{\pi D^4}{32}}{\frac{D}{2}} = \frac{\pi D^3}{16} \approx 0.1963 D^3$$

Torque capacity: $T = \tau_{perm} \cdot Z_p = \frac{\pi}{16} \tau_{perm} D^3$

Hollow Circular Shaft

Outer diameter $D$, inner diameter $d$, and ratio $k = d/D$:

$$J = \frac{\pi (D^4 - d^4)}{32} = \frac{\pi D^4 (1 - k^4)}{32}$$

Polar Section Modulus ($Z_p = J / R_{outer}$):

$$Z_p = \frac{\pi (D^4 - d^4)}{16 D} = \frac{\pi D^3 (1 - k^4)}{16}$$

Note: Divide by $D/2$ (the outer radius where maximum stress occurs), NOT $(D-d)/2$!

Stress Analysis

Shear Stress Distribution & Helical Failure Planes

From the torsion formula $\tau(r) = \frac{\tau_{max}}{R} r$, shear stress varies linearly with the distance from the shaft center. It is strictly zero at the neutral axis (shaft center) and reaches peak magnitude $\tau_{max}$ at the outer perimeter.

Cross-Sectional Stress Profile

Shear stress variation: Zero at center to $\tau_{max}$ at boundary.

State of Pure Shear & Principal Stresses

An infinitesimal element cut at the shaft surface experiences pure shear stresses $\tau$. By transformation of plane stress: $$\sigma_{1} = +\tau \quad (\text{tensile}) \quad \text{at } \theta = +45^\circ$$ $$\sigma_{2} = -\tau \quad (\text{compressive}) \quad \text{at } \theta = -45^\circ$$

Ductile vs. Brittle Failure Modes in Torsion

  • Ductile materials (Mild Steel, Aluminum): Weakest in shear. Failure occurs on a transverse plane perpendicular to the axis ($90^\circ$ flat shear fracture).
  • Brittle materials (Chalk, Cast Iron, Concrete): Much weaker in tension than in shear. Failure occurs along a $45^\circ$ helical surface perpendicular to the maximum principal tensile stress $\sigma_1$.

Deformation & Stiffness

Angle of Twist ($\theta$) & Torsional Rigidity ($GJ$)

From the torsion equation $\frac{T}{J} = \frac{G\theta}{L}$, the total angle of twist over length $L$ is:

$$\theta = \frac{T L}{G J} \quad \text{(in radians)}$$

Warning: $\theta$ from the equation is strictly in radians! To convert to degrees: $\theta^\circ = \theta \times \frac{180}{\pi}$.

Torsional Rigidity

$$GJ$$

Product of shear modulus $G$ and polar inertia $J$. Represents torque required to produce unit angle of twist per unit length ($\text{N}\cdot\text{mm}^2$).

Torsional Stiffness ($k_t$)

$$k_t = \frac{T}{\theta} = \frac{GJ}{L}$$

Torque required to produce unit rotation of the shaft end ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m/rad}$ or $\text{N}\cdot\text{m/deg}$).

Torsional Flexibility ($f_t$)

$$f_t = \frac{1}{k_t} = \frac{L}{GJ}$$

Angle of twist produced per unit applied torque ($\text{rad/N}\cdot\text{m}$).

Dual Design Criteria for Power Transmission Shafts

In real mechanical engineering, a shaft must satisfy two independent design conditions:

1. Strength Criterion:

$\tau_{max} \le [\tau]_{allow} \implies D \ge \left(\frac{16 T}{\pi [\tau]}\right)^{1/3}$

2. Rigidity / Stiffness Criterion:

$\theta \le [\theta]_{allow} \implies D \ge \left(\frac{32 T L}{\pi G [\theta]_{rad}}\right)^{1/4}$

The design diameter must be the LARGER of the two values: $D = \max(D_{strength}, D_{rigidity})$.

Rotational Dynamics

Power Transmission by Rotating Shafts

Shafts are widely utilized to transmit mechanical power in steam/gas turbines, electric motors, automobile transmissions, and machine tools.

Derivation of Power Relationship

Let a shaft rotate at $N$ revolutions per minute (RPM) under constant twisting moment $T$ ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$).

$$\text{Angular velocity } \omega = \frac{2\pi N}{60} \quad \text{(rad/s)}$$ $$\text{Work done per second (Power } P\text{)} = T \cdot \omega = \frac{2\pi N T}{60} \quad \text{Watts (W)}$$ $$\text{In Kilowatts: } P = \frac{2\pi N T}{60 \times 1000} \text{ kW} \implies T = \frac{60,000 \cdot P}{2\pi N} \quad \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$$

Peak Torque vs. Mean Torque

Engines and machinery experience fluctuations. Peak torque $T_{max}$ exceeds mean torque $T_{mean}$:

$$T_{max} = k \times T_{mean}$$

Always design the shaft for $T_{max}$! Sizing for $T_{mean}$ leads to premature fatigue failure.

Quick Sizing Shortcut

For a solid shaft with allowable shear stress $\tau_{allow}$:

$$D = \sqrt[3]{\frac{16 T_{max}}{\pi \tau_{allow}}}$$

Ensure units match: If $T$ is in $\text{N}\cdot\text{mm}$ and $\tau$ is in $\text{MPa}$ ($\text{N/mm}^2$), $D$ is in $\text{mm}$.

Engineering Optimization

Comparison of Solid vs. Hollow Shafts

Why are aerospace drive shafts and marine propeller shafts almost universally hollow? Let's prove their superior efficiency mathematically across three classical engineering scenarios:

Scenario 1: Same Strength ($T$ and $\tau_{max}$ are identical)

Let solid shaft diameter be $D_s$. For a hollow shaft with outer diameter $D_h$ and inner diameter $d_h = k D_h$:

$$T = \frac{\pi}{16} \tau D_s^3 = \frac{\pi}{16} \tau D_h^3 (1 - k^4) \implies D_h = \frac{D_s}{(1 - k^4)^{1/3}}$$ $$\text{Weight Ratio: } \frac{W_h}{W_s} = \frac{A_h}{A_s} = \frac{D_h^2(1 - k^2)}{D_s^2} = \frac{1 - k^2}{(1 - k^4)^{2/3}}$$
Material Saving Demonstration: If diameter ratio $k = 0.6$: $$\frac{W_h}{W_s} = \frac{1 - 0.6^2}{(1 - 0.6^4)^{2/3}} = \frac{0.64}{(0.8704)^{2/3}} = \frac{0.64}{0.9118} = 0.702 \implies \mathbf{29.8\% \text{ weight saved!}}$$ If $k = 0.8$, weight saving reaches $\mathbf{44.6\%}$!

Scenario 2: Same Weight (Cross-sectional areas are identical)

Since areas are equal, $D_s^2 = D_h^2(1 - k^2) \implies D_h = \frac{D_s}{\sqrt{1 - k^2}}$. Comparing torque capacities:

$$\frac{T_h}{T_s} = \frac{Z_{ph}}{Z_{ps}} = \frac{D_h^3(1 - k^4)}{D_s^3} = \frac{1 - k^4}{(1 - k^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{1 + k^2}{\sqrt{1 - k^2}}$$

For $k = 0.6$: $\frac{T_h}{T_s} = \frac{1 + 0.36}{\sqrt{1 - 0.36}} = \frac{1.36}{0.8} = \mathbf{1.70}$. The hollow shaft is 70% stronger for the exact same amount of metal!

Physical Intuition: Material near the center of a solid shaft experiences near-zero stress ($\tau \approx 0$) and has a tiny moment arm, contributing almost nothing to resisting torque. A hollow shaft moves that same mass to outer radii where both shear stress and moment arm are maximized!

Structural Assemblies

Shafts in Series & Stepped Shafts

When two or more shaft segments of different diameters or materials are connected end-to-end to transmit torque from one end to another, they are said to be in series.

Equilibrium Condition

The internal torque transmitted across every transverse section along the length is identical:

$$T_1 = T_2 = \dots = T_n = T$$

Maximum shear stress always occurs in the segment with the SMALLEST diameter! $$\tau_{max} = \frac{16 T}{\pi D_{min}^3}$$

Kinematic Compatibility

The total angle of twist across the ends is the algebraic sum of the individual twists of each segment:

$$\theta_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^n \theta_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{T L_i}{G_i J_i}$$

Analogous to springs in series: Compliance is additive ($1/k_{eq} = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2$).

Stepped shaft in series: $T$ is common; $\theta = \frac{T L_1}{G_1 J_1} + \frac{T L_2}{G_2 J_2}$.

Parallel Assemblies

Shafts in Parallel & Concentric Composite Shafts

Shafts are in parallel when they are coupled such that the angle of twist is identical for all components, while the total applied torque is shared among them.

Kinematic Compatibility

Both components twist through the exact same angle:

$$\theta_1 = \theta_2 = \theta \implies \frac{T_1 L_1}{G_1 J_1} = \frac{T_2 L_2}{G_2 J_2}$$

If lengths are equal ($L_1 = L_2$): $\frac{T_1}{T_2} = \frac{G_1 J_1}{G_2 J_2}$ (torque shares proportional to torsional rigidity!).

Torque Equilibrium

Total externally applied torque equals sum of resisting torques:

$$T = T_1 + T_2$$ $$T_1 = T \left[\frac{G_1 J_1}{G_1 J_1 + G_2 J_2}\right], \quad T_2 = T \left[\frac{G_2 J_2}{G_1 J_1 + G_2 J_2}\right]$$
Concentric Composite Shaft (e.g. Steel Core inside Brass Sleeve):

Because the two materials are rigidly bonded at the interface ($r = R_{core}$), there is no slip. The shear strain at the interface is identical: $\gamma = R_{core} \theta / L$. However, the shear stresses differ because shear moduli differ: $\tau_{steel} / G_{steel} = \tau_{brass} / G_{brass}$.

Advanced Equilibrium

Statically Indeterminate Torsion Problems

When a shaft is rigidly fixed at both ends $A$ and $B$, and subjected to an intermediate twisting moment $T_C$ at point $C$, static equilibrium alone cannot determine the fixing reactions $T_A$ and $T_B$.

Step 1: Equation of Static Equilibrium

Sum of torques about the longitudinal axis must vanish:

$$T_A + T_B = T_C \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

1 equation with 2 unknown reactions $\implies$ Degree of static indeterminacy = 1.

Step 2: Compatibility of Deformation

Because both ends $A$ and $B$ are fixed to rigid walls, the total angle of twist between $A$ and $B$ is zero:

$$\theta_{AB} = \theta_{AC} + \theta_{CB} = 0 \implies \frac{T_A L_1}{G_1 J_1} - \frac{T_B L_2}{G_2 J_2} = 0 \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

Step 3: Closed-Form Reaction Formulas

$$T_A = T_C \cdot \frac{L_2 / (G_2 J_2)}{\frac{L_1}{G_1 J_1} + \frac{L_2}{G_2 J_2}}, \quad T_B = T_C \cdot \frac{L_1 / (G_1 J_1)}{\frac{L_1}{G_1 J_1} + \frac{L_2}{G_2 J_2}}$$

For a uniform shaft ($G_1 J_1 = G_2 J_2$): $T_A = T_C \frac{L_2}{L}$ and $T_B = T_C \frac{L_1}{L}$. The reaction at each end is inversely proportional to its distance from the load!

Energy Principles

Strain Energy in Torsion & Helical Springs

When torque gradually increases from zero to $T$, producing angle of twist $\theta$, work done by external couple equals the elastic strain energy stored in the shaft:

Strain Energy in Pure Torsion

$$U = \frac{1}{2} T \theta = \frac{T^2 L}{2GJ} = \frac{GJ \theta^2}{2L}$$

In terms of maximum surface shear stress $\tau$:

$$\text{Solid Shaft: } U = \frac{\tau^2}{4G} \times \text{Volume}$$ $$\text{Hollow Shaft: } U = \frac{\tau^2}{4G} \left[\frac{D^2 + d^2}{D^2}\right] \times \text{Volume}$$

Torsional Modulus of Resilience = $\frac{\tau_{yield}^2}{4G}$ (half of direct shear strain energy density!).

Closely-Coiled Helical Springs

An axial force $W$ applied to a closely-coiled spring of wire diameter $d$, mean coil radius $R$, and $n$ turns subjects the wire to pure torsion of magnitude $T = W \times R$:

$$\text{Shear Stress: } \tau = \frac{16 W R}{\pi d^3}$$ $$\text{Axial Deflection: } \delta = \frac{64 W R^3 n}{G d^4}$$ $$\text{Spring Stiffness: } k = \frac{W}{\delta} = \frac{G d^4}{64 R^3 n}$$

Multiaxial Stresses

Combined Bending and Torsion in Power Shafts

Real transmission shafts carry pulleys, gears, belts, and sprockets, subjecting the shaft simultaneously to bending moment $M$ and twisting moment $T$.

State of Stress at the Critical Outer Fiber

$$\sigma_b = \frac{32 M}{\pi D^3} \quad \text{(Bending Normal Stress)}$$
$$\tau = \frac{16 T}{\pi D^3} \quad \text{(Torsional Shear Stress)}$$

Guest's (Maximum Shear Stress) Theory

Standard design theory for ductile materials (mild steel, alloy steels):

$$\tau_{max} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_b}{2}\right)^2 + \tau^2} = \frac{16}{\pi D^3} \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}$$

Equivalent Twisting Moment ($T_e$):

$$T_e = \sqrt{M^2 + T^2} \implies D = \sqrt[3]{\frac{16 T_e}{\pi [\tau]}}$$

Rankine's (Maximum Principal Stress) Theory

Design theory for brittle materials (cast iron):

$$\sigma_1 = \frac{\sigma_b}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_b}{2}\right)^2 + \tau^2} = \frac{16}{\pi D^3} \left[M + \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}\right]$$

Equivalent Bending Moment ($M_e$):

$$M_e = \frac{1}{2}\left[M + \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}\right] \implies D = \sqrt[3]{\frac{32 M_e}{\pi [\sigma]}}$$
