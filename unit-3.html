Module 3

Why bending stress decides the beam

Unit 2 ended with a number: the maximum bending moment. That number tells you nothing about whether the beam survives. A moment of 96 kN·m is trivial for a plate girder and fatal for a 100 × 200 timber joist. What converts the demand into a verdict is the section.

The whole unit in one line

$$ \sigma_b = \frac{M}{Z} \le \sigma_{permissible} $$

Everything else — the bending equation, moment of inertia, section modulus, the shear formula — exists to produce \(Z\) honestly for a section of any shape, and then to check that the shear does not govern instead.

Four sections, and the reason each looks the way it does

The observation that starts the unit

Take a 100 mm × 200 mm timber joist. Lay it flat and it carries a certain moment. Stand it on edge — same material, same weight per metre, same cost — and it carries twice as much.

Flat: Z = bd²/6 = 200 × 100² / 6 = 333 × 10³ mm³

On edge: Z = 100 × 200² / 6 = 667 × 10³ mm³

Ratio = 2.0 — for nothing but a rotation

Depth appears squared in the section modulus, width only to the first power. That single fact explains why joists stand on edge, why beams are deeper than they are wide, and why the I-section exists at all.

Module 4

Theory of simple bending

Simple or pure bending means a length of beam carrying constant bending moment and zero shear force. The central third of a beam under two symmetric point loads is the standard laboratory realisation — Unit 2, Module 11.

The six assumptions

AssumptionWhy it is neededWhere it fails
Plane sections remain plane after bendingMakes strain vary linearly with distance from the neutral axisDeep beams (span/depth < 2), near point loads
The material is homogeneous and isotropicOne value of E throughoutTimber, RCC, composites — Module 15 handles these
The material obeys Hooke's lawConverts linear strain into linear stressBeyond the proportional limit; plastic design
E is the same in tension and compressionPuts the neutral axis at the centroidCast iron, concrete — the NA shifts
The beam is initially straight, with constant cross-sectionRadius of curvature is due to the load aloneCurved beams, tapered members
Every layer is free to expand or contract independentlyNo lateral restraint between fibresVery wide flanges, anticlastic curvature

A seventh is sometimes listed — that the transverse section is symmetrical about the plane of bending. Without it the beam twists as well as bends, which is unsymmetrical bending and a later topic.

What "plane sections remain plane" buys you

It is the assumption that does all the work. If a flat section stays flat as the beam curves, then the stretch of any fibre is proportional to its distance from the layer that does not change length. Strain is therefore linear in \(y\), and by Hooke's law so is stress.

Note what is not assumed: nothing about the shape of the cross-section. That is why one derivation covers rectangles, circles, I-sections and anything else you can compute an \(I\) for.

Module 5

Deriving the bending equation

Step 1 — strain varies linearly with y

Take an element of length \(dx\) that bends to a radius \(R\), subtending an angle \(d\theta\) at the centre of curvature. The neutral layer NN does not change length, so \(dx = R\,d\theta\).

A fibre at distance \(y\) below the neutral layer has original length \(dx\) and final length \((R+y)\,d\theta\).

$$ \varepsilon = \frac{(R+y)d\theta - R\,d\theta}{R\,d\theta} = \frac{y}{R} $$

Step 2 — stress varies linearly too

Apply Hooke's law, \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\):

$$ \sigma = \frac{E\,y}{R} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\sigma}{y} = \frac{E}{R} $$

Two of the three terms of the bending equation are already in place, and neither depended on the shape of the section.

Step 3 — the internal moment

Take an elemental area \(dA\) at distance \(y\). The force on it is \(\sigma\,dA = (Ey/R)dA\), and its moment about the neutral axis is \(y\) times that:

$$ M = \int \frac{E y^2}{R}\,dA = \frac{E}{R}\int y^2 dA = \frac{E I}{R} $$

The integral \(\int y^2 dA\) is the second moment of area \(I\) about the neutral axis. It appears not because anyone chose it but because the algebra produced it — \(I\) is simply what a shape contributes when every fibre's force is weighted by its own lever arm.

Step 4 — combine

$$ \frac{M}{I} = \frac{\sigma}{y} = \frac{E}{R} $$
M/I = σ/y
Gives the stress at any fibre — the design relation.
σ/y = E/R
Links stress to curvature — used in the laboratory to measure E.
M/I = E/R
Gives curvature from moment — the starting point of Unit 4, deflection.

Module 6

The neutral axis

The neutral layer is the surface whose fibres neither stretch nor shorten. Its trace on the cross-section is the neutral axis. Everything above it is in compression, everything below in tension, and the stress is zero on it.

Proof that it passes through the centroid

In pure bending there is no axial force, so the resultant of all the stresses on the section must vanish:

$$ \int \sigma\,dA = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \int \frac{Ey}{R}dA = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{E}{R}\int y\,dA = 0 $$

Since \(E/R \ne 0\), we need \(\int y\,dA = 0\). That integral is the first moment of area about the axis, and it is zero only about an axis through the centroid. Hence the neutral axis passes through the centroid of the section — for a homogeneous beam in pure bending.

Read the qualifiers. If E differs in tension and compression, or the section is made of two materials, or an axial force acts alongside the moment, the neutral axis moves off the centroid. Module 15 works through the two-material case.

Locating the centroid of a built-up section

$$ \bar{y} = \frac{\sum A_i \bar{y}_i}{\sum A_i} $$

Measure all \(\bar y_i\) from one convenient datum — the bottom edge is the usual choice — and keep the datum for the whole calculation. For a section symmetric about a horizontal axis the centroid sits at mid-depth and no arithmetic is needed; say so and move on.

Worked example — a T-section

Flange 150 mm × 25 mm on top, web 25 mm × 150 mm below it. Total depth 175 mm. Locate the neutral axis from the bottom.

Web: A₁ = 25 × 150 = 3750 mm², ȳ₁ = 75 mm

Flange: A₂ = 150 × 25 = 3750 mm², ȳ₂ = 150 + 12.5 = 162.5 mm

ΣA = 7500 mm²

ȳ = (3750 × 75 + 3750 × 162.5) / 7500

ȳ = (281 250 + 609 375) / 7500 = 118.75 mm from the bottom

Distance to the top fibre = 175 − 118.75 = 56.25 mm

The neutral axis is nowhere near mid-depth. That asymmetry is the whole point of a T-section: the tension fibre is 118.75 mm away and the compression fibre only 56.25 mm, so the section develops far more tensile stress than compressive. Useful for cast iron, which is weak in tension — turn the T upside down and the strong material sits where it is needed.

Module 7

Moment of inertia

The second moment of area \(I = \int y^2 dA\) measures how far the material sits from the bending axis. Because the distance is squared, material near the axis contributes almost nothing and material at the extremes contributes enormously. That single fact drives the whole of section design.

Parallel axis theorem

$$ I_{AB} = I_G + A h^2 $$

\(I_G\) is about the shape's own centroidal axis, \(h\) is the distance from that axis to the axis you want, and \(A\) is the area. Every built-up section is handled by splitting it into standard parts and shifting each one to the common neutral axis.

The \(Ah^2\) term usually dwarfs \(I_G\). For a thin flange far from the axis, \(I_G\) is often under 2 % of its total contribution — but leave it out and the answer is still wrong, and examiners look for it.

Worked example — I of the T-section from Module 6

Flange 150 × 25 on top, web 25 × 150. We found ȳ = 118.75 mm from the bottom.

Web: IG = 25 × 150³/12 = 7.031 × 10⁶ mm⁴, h = 118.75 − 75 = 43.75 mm

        A h² = 3750 × 43.75² = 7.178 × 10⁶ mm⁴

Flange: IG = 150 × 25³/12 = 0.195 × 10⁶ mm⁴, h = 162.5 − 118.75 = 43.75 mm

        A h² = 3750 × 43.75² = 7.178 × 10⁶ mm⁴

I = 7.031 + 7.178 + 0.195 + 7.178 = 21.58 × 10⁶ mm⁴

Notice the flange: its own IG is 0.195 × 10⁶ but its shift term is 7.178 × 10⁶ — thirty-seven times larger. That is the parallel axis theorem earning its place.

Module 8

Section modulus

The maximum bending stress occurs at the fibre furthest from the neutral axis, \(y = y_{max}\). Putting that into the bending equation:

$$ \sigma_{max} = \frac{M\,y_{max}}{I} = \frac{M}{Z} \qquad\text{where}\qquad Z = \frac{I}{y_{max}} $$

Section modulus \(Z\) collapses the whole geometry of a section into one number with units of mm³. Two sections with the same \(Z\) carry the same moment at the same stress, whatever they look like. It is the number steel tables are organised around and the number you compute first in any design.

Symmetric and unsymmetric sections

Symmetric about the NA

One value of Z serves both faces, since \(y_{top} = y_{bot} = d/2\). Rectangle, circle, symmetric I-section.

Unsymmetric

Two values: \(Z_{top} = I/y_{top}\) and \(Z_{bot} = I/y_{bot}\). The smaller Z governs, because that face reaches the permissible stress first. T-sections, channels, angles.

For the T-section of Modules 6 and 7: \(Z_{bot} = 21.58\times10^6 / 118.75 = 181.7 \times 10^3\) mm³ and \(Z_{top} = 21.58\times10^6/56.25 = 383.7 \times 10^3\) mm³. The bottom governs — it is more than twice as critical.

Module 9

Bending stress distribution

Because \(\sigma = My/I\) and \(M\) and \(I\) are constant across a given section, the stress varies linearly with y. Zero at the neutral axis, maximum at the extreme fibres, compression on one side and tension on the other.

Shape of the diagram
Always two straight lines meeting at zero on the NA — regardless of the shape of the cross-section.
Sagging moment
Compression above the NA, tension below.
Hogging moment
Tension above, compression below. The diagram simply flips.

The mistake this module exists to prevent

Students routinely draw a stress diagram that follows the outline of the section — stepping in and out at the flange of an I-beam. It does not. The bending stress diagram is a straight line in \(y\) whatever the width does, because width never appears in \(\sigma = My/I\). Width affects \(I\), and therefore the magnitude, but not the linearity.

The shear stress diagram in Module 12 is the opposite case: it does jump where the width changes. Keeping the two straight is the main conceptual hurdle of this unit.

Worked example

A 150 mm wide × 300 mm deep rectangular beam carries a maximum sagging moment of 96 kN·m. Find the extreme fibre stresses and the stress 50 mm below the top.

I = bd³/12 = 150 × 300³/12 = 337.5 × 10⁶ mm⁴

ymax = 150 mm, so Z = 337.5×10⁶/150 = 2.25 × 10⁶ mm³

M = 96 kN·m = 96 × 10⁶ N·mm

σmax = M/Z = 96×10⁶ / 2.25×10⁶ = 42.67 MPa

Top fibre: 42.67 MPa compressive. Bottom fibre: 42.67 MPa tensile.

At 50 mm below the top, y = 150 − 50 = 100 mm from the NA

σ = My/I = 96×10⁶ × 100 / 337.5×10⁶ = 28.44 MPa compressive

Check by proportion: 42.67 × (100/150) = 28.44 ✓

Module 10

Why beams are I-shaped

Bending stress is proportional to distance from the neutral axis. Material sitting on the neutral axis carries no bending stress at all — it contributes weight and cost and nothing else. The economical section therefore pushes area to the extreme fibres and keeps only enough in the middle to hold the two flanges together and carry the shear.

The comparison in numbers

All three sections below have the same area, 5808 mm², so the same weight of steel per metre.

SectionDimensionsI (mm⁴)Z (mm³)Relative capacity
Square76.2 × 76.22.81 × 10⁶73.8 × 10³1.00
Rectangle on edge48 × 1217.09 × 10⁶117 × 10³1.59
I-section150 flange, 8 web, 300 deep88.7 × 10⁶591 × 10³8.01

Eight times the moment capacity for the same steel. That ratio is the entire commercial justification for rolling I-sections rather than selling bar stock.

Where the limits are

  • The web cannot be made arbitrarily thin. It carries nearly all the shear (Module 13) and it buckles if it is too slender. IS 800 sets limiting width-to-thickness ratios for exactly this reason.
  • Deep sections buckle laterally. A very deep, narrow beam can twist and buckle sideways long before the bending stress reaches the permissible value, unless the compression flange is restrained.
  • Deflection may govern instead of stress. Deflection depends on \(I\), not \(Z\), and a section that satisfies stress can still sag visibly. Unit 4 handles that check.
  • The idea is not new. A hollow bone, a bamboo culm and a steel tube all place material away from the axis for the same reason. Nature reached the I-section first.

Module 11

Shear stress in beams

Bending moment produces bending stress. The shear force from Unit 2 produces shear stress, and it is distributed in a completely different way — largest where the bending stress is zero, and zero where the bending stress is largest.

Derivation

  1. Take an element of length \(dx\). The moment on the left face is \(M\), on the right face \(M + dM\).
  2. Consider only the part of the section above a level \(y_1\), of area \(A\).
  3. The bending force on the left face of that area is \(\int (My/I)dA = \dfrac{M}{I}A\bar y\), and on the right face \(\dfrac{M+dM}{I}A\bar y\).
  4. These are unequal, so the difference must be balanced by a horizontal shear force on the plane at \(y_1\), of area \(b\,dx\):
$$ \tau \, b \, dx = \frac{dM}{I}A\bar{y} \;\Longrightarrow\; \tau = \frac{dM}{dx}\cdot\frac{A\bar y}{I b} $$

and since \(dM/dx = V\) from Unit 2:

$$ \tau = \frac{V A \bar{y}}{I b} $$

What each symbol means — read carefully

SymbolMeaningWhere students go wrong
VShear force at the section, from the SFDUsing the bending moment instead
AArea of the section above (or below) the level considered, not the whole sectionUsing the total area — the single commonest error in this unit
ȳDistance from the neutral axis to the centroid of that area AUsing the distance to the level itself
ISecond moment of area of the whole section about the NAUsing only the part above the level
bWidth of the section at the level where τ is wantedUsing the flange width when the level is in the web

The product \(A\bar y\) is the first moment of area of the part above the level, taken about the neutral axis. It is often written \(Q\), and \(\tau = VQ/Ib\) is the same formula.

Complementary shear — why a horizontal stress appears at all

The shear force is vertical, so students reasonably ask where a horizontal shear stress comes from. The answer is that shear stresses always occur in complementary pairs: a vertical shear stress on a vertical plane is impossible without an equal horizontal shear stress on the horizontal plane, or the element would spin.

A stack of loose planks bent over your knee demonstrates this: the planks slide past one another at their interfaces. Glue them and the glue must resist that sliding — the glue carries exactly the horizontal shear stress this formula predicts. That is also why a timber beam can fail by splitting along its length rather than snapping across it.

Module 12

Shear stress in a rectangular section

Derivation of the parabola

For a rectangle of width \(b\) and depth \(d\), take a level at distance \(y\) from the neutral axis. The area above it is \(A = b\left(\frac{d}{2}-y\right)\), and its centroid is at \(\bar y = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}+y\right)\) from the NA.

$$ A\bar y = \frac{b}{2}\left(\frac{d^2}{4} - y^2\right) $$

Substituting into \(\tau = VA\bar y/Ib\) with \(I = bd^3/12\):

$$ \tau = \frac{6V}{bd^3}\left(\frac{d^2}{4} - y^2\right) $$

The stress is parabolic in \(y\): zero at the top and bottom fibres where \(y = \pm d/2\), and maximum at the neutral axis where \(y = 0\).

The 1.5 result

$$ \tau_{max} = \frac{6V}{bd^3}\cdot\frac{d^2}{4} = \frac{3V}{2bd} = 1.5\,\tau_{average} $$

The maximum shear stress in a rectangular section is 1.5 times the average. Anyone who designs on \(V/A\) alone underestimates the peak by a third, which is why this factor is quoted in every code and asked in every examination.

Worked example

The 150 × 300 beam of Module 9 carries a shear force of 60 kN at the same section. Find the average and maximum shear stress, and the stress at 75 mm above the neutral axis.

A = 150 × 300 = 45 000 mm²

τavg = V/A = 60 000 / 45 000 = 1.33 MPa

τmax = 1.5 × 1.33 = 2.00 MPa at the neutral axis

At y = 75 mm: τ = (6 × 60 000)/(150 × 300³) × (300²/4 − 75²)

τ = 8.889×10⁻⁵ × (22 500 − 5625) = 8.889×10⁻⁵ × 16 875 = 1.50 MPa

Check by the parabola: 2.00 × [1 − (75/150)²] = 2.00 × 0.75 = 1.50 ✓

Compare the two stresses at this section: bending 42.67 MPa, shear 2.00 MPa. In an ordinary beam of reasonable span the bending stress dominates by an order of magnitude, which is why bending governs design and shear is a check. Shear becomes critical only for short deep beams, heavily loaded near supports, or timber, where the material is weak along the grain.

Module 13

Shear distribution in other sections

Circular section

At the neutral axis \(A\bar y = \frac{2}{3}r^3\), \(I = \pi r^4/4\) and \(b = 2r\), giving:

$$ \tau_{max} = \frac{4}{3}\,\tau_{average} = \frac{4V}{3\pi r^2} $$

Still parabolic in shape but with a lower peak factor than the rectangle, because the width of a circle is greatest exactly where the first moment is greatest.

I-section — the important case

The distribution is parabolic within the flange and parabolic within the web, but there is a sudden jump at the junction. Nothing physical changes there — \(A\bar y\) is continuous. What changes is \(b\), from the flange width to the web thickness, and \(\tau\) is inversely proportional to \(b\).

For a 300 deep section, 150 × 12 flanges, 8 mm web, carrying V = 50 kN:

I = 88.71 × 10⁶ mm⁴

At the junction, A ȳ = 150 × 12 × 144 = 259 200 mm³

In the flange (b = 150): τ = 50 000 × 259 200 / (88.71×10⁶ × 150) = 0.97 MPa

In the web (b = 8): τ = 50 000 × 259 200 / (88.71×10⁶ × 8) = 18.26 MPa

The jump factor is exactly 150/8 = 18.75

Peak at the neutral axis: A ȳ = 259 200 + 8 × 138 × 69 = 335 376 mm³

τmax = 50 000 × 335 376 / (88.71×10⁶ × 8) = 23.63 MPa

Why the web carries the shear and the flanges carry the moment

Integrating the distribution over the section recovers the full 50 kN, and 96 % of it is carried by the web alone. The flanges, meanwhile, carry the great majority of the moment. This clean division of labour is why steel design treats the two separately, using the quick approximation

$$ \tau_{avg,\,web} \approx \frac{V}{d\,t_w} $$

For the section above that gives 50 000/(300 × 8) = 20.83 MPa, against an exact peak at the neutral axis of 23.63 MPa. The approximation is therefore about 12 % unconservative — acceptable for a preliminary check because the permissible shear stress carries its own margin, but not a substitute for the exact value when the web is highly stressed. Quote both if a question asks you to comment.

Module 14

Bending and shear together

At most sections both stresses act at once. Their distributions are complementary in the most literal sense: where one is maximum, the other is zero.

Position in the sectionBending stress σShear stress τ
Extreme fibres (top and bottom)maximumzero
Neutral axiszeromaximum
Intermediatelinear in yparabolic in y

Along the length of the beam the same complementarity appears: at a simple support the shear is maximum and the moment zero, while at mid-span of a UDL beam the moment is maximum and the shear zero.

Which one governs?

  • Long, lightly loaded beams: bending governs. Typical span-to-depth ratios above about 10.
  • Short, heavily loaded beams: shear governs. \(M\) grows with span but \(V\) does not, so as the span shortens shear catches up.
  • Timber: shear frequently governs, because timber is weak in shear parallel to the grain — often only 1 to 2 MPa permissible against 10 or more in bending.
  • Thin-webbed plate girders: shear and web buckling govern, which is why stiffeners are welded to the web at intervals.

A note on principal stresses

At a point away from both the neutral axis and the extreme fibre, \(\sigma\) and \(\tau\) act together, and the true maximum stress is the principal stress obtained by combining them — the Mohr's circle work of Unit 1. In routine beam design the two are checked separately because the critical points for each are at different places, but for a plate girder web the combination is checked explicitly.

$$ \sigma_{1,2} = \frac{\sigma}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)^2 + \tau^2} $$

Module 15

Composite and flitched beams

A flitched beam is a timber joist with steel plates bolted to its sides; an RCC beam is concrete with steel bars. In both cases two materials of different \(E\) are rigidly joined and bend as one member.

The governing condition

Being bonded, the two materials bend to the same radius of curvature.

$$ R_1 = R_2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{E_1 I_1}{M_1} = \frac{E_2 I_2}{M_2} $$

Consequences

At any level \(y\) the strains are equal, so the stresses are in the ratio of the moduli:

$$ \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2} = \frac{E_1}{E_2} = m $$

\(m\) is the modular ratio. The stiffer material attracts stress in proportion to its modulus — the same principle as the composite bar of Unit 1, Module 13, now applied to bending instead of axial load.

The equivalent section method

  1. Choose one material as the reference — usually the weaker one, timber.
  2. Multiply the width of every other material by \(m = E_{steel}/E_{timber}\). Never change the depth: depth controls \(y\), and \(y\) must stay put for the strains to match.
  3. Compute \(I\) of the transformed section as if it were made entirely of the reference material.
  4. Find the stress in the reference material from \(\sigma = My/I_{eq}\).
  5. Multiply by \(m\) to obtain the stress in the transformed material.

Worked example

A timber joist 100 mm wide × 200 mm deep is reinforced with two steel plates 10 mm thick × 200 mm deep, one on each side. \(E_{steel} = 200\) GPa, \(E_{timber} = 10\) GPa. The beam carries a moment of 30 kN·m. Find the maximum stress in each material and the share of the moment.

Modular ratio m = 200/10 = 20

Equivalent timber width of each plate = 20 × 10 = 200 mm

Transformed section = timber 100 wide + two strips 200 wide, all 200 deep

Ieq = (100 + 200 + 200) × 200³/12 = 500 × 200³/12 = 333.3 × 10⁶ mm⁴

ymax = 100 mm

σtimber = My/Ieq = 30×10⁶ × 100 / 333.3×10⁶ = 9.0 MPa

σsteel = m × σtimber = 20 × 9.0 = 180.0 MPa

Moment shared in proportion to EI:

Itimber = 100 × 200³/12 = 66.67 × 10⁶ mm⁴ → Mt = 30 × (66.67/333.3) = 6.0 kN·m

Msteel = 30 − 6 = 24.0 kN·m

The steel is only 29 % of the cross-sectional area but carries 80 % of the moment. That is what a modular ratio of 20 does, and it is the reason a modest pair of plates transforms a timber joist.

The spine of Unit 3: pure bending makes plane sections rotate, so strain and therefore stress vary linearly with distance from the neutral axis; integrating that stress over the section produces I and the bending equation; dividing I by the extreme distance gives Z, and M/Z is the stress that must be checked. The shear branch runs in parallel from the same element, and composite beams are the whole chain applied twice with a modular ratio linking them.

Level 6 — Case study

Timber floor joist with steel plates

A timber joist 100 mm wide and 250 mm deep spans 4 m and carries a UDL of 6 kN/m including its own weight. Permissible stresses are 8 MPa in bending and 1.0 MPa in shear parallel to the grain. If the timber alone is inadequate, two mild steel plates 8 mm thick and 250 mm deep are to be bolted one to each side. Take Etimber = 10 GPa and Esteel = 200 GPa.
