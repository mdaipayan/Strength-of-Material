Module 3

Why deflection is checked at all

A beam can be perfectly safe in bending and shear and still be unacceptable. Units 2 and 3 answered "will it break?". This unit answers a different question: "will anyone accept it?" — and the two failure modes are governed by different limits and often by different members.

Strength — ultimate limit state

Collapse. Governed by σ = M/Z and τ. Consequence: the structure falls down.

Serviceability — deflection

Cracked plaster, doors that jam, ponding on roofs, bouncy floors, cracked partitions, alarmed occupants. The structure stands, and is still rejected.

Code limits

CodeSituationLimit
IS 456 (concrete)Total deflection, including creep and shrinkagespan / 250
IS 456 (concrete)Deflection occurring after partitions are builtspan / 350 or 20 mm, whichever is less
IS 800 (steel)Live-load deflection, floors and roofs, elastic claddingspan / 240 to span / 360, by element
IS 800 (steel)Members supporting brittle finishesTighter — brittle finishes crack early

IS 800:2007 Table 6 sets different limits for different members and cladding types, and the codes are revised. Quote the table for the case in hand rather than a single remembered number — an examiner accepts "span/250 by IS 456" but a design office wants the right row of the right table.

A worked reality check

Take the 8 m simply supported beam of Unit 3: 150 × 300 rectangular, carrying 12 kN/m, with E = 200 GPa.

I = 337.5 × 10⁶ mm⁴, EI = 200 000 × 337.5×10⁶ = 6.75 × 10¹³ N·mm²

δ = 5wL⁴/(384EI) = 5 × 12 × 8000⁴ / (384 × 6.75×10¹³)

δ = 9.48 mm at mid-span

Limit by IS 456, span/250 = 8000/250 = 32 mm → satisfactory

Limit for brittle finishes, span/350 = 22.9 mm → still satisfactory

Now halve the depth to 150 mm. I falls by a factor of eight, so the deflection becomes 75.9 mm — more than twice the limit — while the bending stress only quadruples. Deflection is the more sensitive check, because it depends on I rather than Z, and I carries depth cubed rather than squared.

Module 4

The elastic curve

When a beam bends, the neutral axis takes up a curved shape called the elastic curve or deflection curve. Two quantities describe it at any section:

Deflection, y

The vertical movement of a point on the neutral axis from its original position. Measured in mm.

Slope, θ

The angle the tangent to the elastic curve makes with the original axis. Measured in radians, and equal to dy/dx.

The small-deflection assumption

Real beam deflections are tiny: 9.5 mm over an 8 m span is a slope of the order of 0.003 radians. That smallness is what makes the mathematics tractable.

$$ \frac{1}{R} = \frac{d^2y/dx^2}{\left[1 + (dy/dx)^2\right]^{3/2}} \;\approx\; \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} $$

Since \(dy/dx\) is around 0.003, its square is about \(10^{-5}\) and the denominator is 1.000 to five decimal places. Dropping it is not a rough approximation — it is exact to well beyond engineering precision, provided the deflections stay small. For a fishing rod or a leaf spring it is not, and large-deflection theory is needed.

The chain from load to deflection

Module 5

The differential equation of the elastic curve

From the bending equation

Unit 3 gave, for a beam in pure bending:

$$ \frac{M}{I} = \frac{E}{R} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{1}{R} = \frac{M}{EI} $$

Curvature \(1/R\) is a purely geometric property of the curve, and for small slopes it equals \(d^2y/dx^2\). Substituting:

$$ EI\,\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = M $$

This is the differential equation of the elastic curve, and it is the entire theoretical content of the unit. Everything after it is integration and bookkeeping.

Integrating twice

First integration → slope

$$ EI\,\frac{dy}{dx} = \int M\,dx + C_1 $$

Second integration → deflection

$$ EI\,y = \iint M\,dx\,dx + C_1x + C_2 $$

Two integrations produce two constants, and two constants demand two known conditions. Supplying them is the subject of the next module — and it is where most marks are lost, not in the integration itself.

The family of relations

Differentiating the elastic curve equation reproduces the whole of Unit 2, which is a useful check that the two units are consistent:

DerivativeEqualsMeaning

Some textbooks start from \(EI\,d^4y/dx^4 = -w\) and integrate four times. That works but needs four boundary conditions; starting from the moment is quicker when the SFD and BMD are already in hand from Unit 2.

Module 6

Sign convention and boundary conditions

Sign convention

Carrying Unit 2's convention forward: sagging moment positive, and y measured positive upward from the original axis.

Consequence for a sagging beam

M is positive, so \(d^2y/dx^2\) is positive, so the curve is concave upward — a smile. The mid-span y comes out negative, meaning downward.

Reporting the answer

A negative y is normal and correct. Report it as "12.4 mm downward" rather than "−12.4 mm" and state the convention once at the top.

Boundary conditions by support type

Support or conditionGivesWhy
Simple support (pin or roller)y = 0Cannot move vertically, but is free to rotate
Fixed supporty = 0 and θ = 0Cannot move and cannot rotate — two conditions from one support
Free endnothingBoth y and θ are unknown there; that is what you are solving for
Point of symmetryθ = 0The tangent is horizontal at mid-span of a symmetrically loaded beam
Internal hingey continuous, θ may jumpThe two spans share a point but not a tangent

Matching conditions to beams

Cantilever fixed at A, free at B

Both conditions come from A: y = 0 and θ = 0 at x = 0. If x is measured from the fixed end, both constants come out zero, which is why cantilever problems are the shortest in the unit.

Simply supported, span L

y = 0 at x = 0 and y = 0 at x = L. If the loading is symmetric you may replace the second with θ = 0 at mid-span, which is usually quicker.

Overhanging beam

y = 0 at each of the two supports. Note that y is not zero at the free end of the overhang — that value is often exactly what the question asks for.

Propped cantilever

Three conditions available — y = 0 and θ = 0 at the fixed end, y = 0 at the prop — but only two constants. The third condition determines the unknown prop reaction, which is how deflection theory solves a statically indeterminate beam.

Module 7

The double integration method

The procedure

  1. Find the reactions.
  2. Take a section at distance x and write \(M_x\) as a function of x, keeping the section general enough to cover the region.
  3. Write \(EI\,d^2y/dx^2 = M_x\).
  4. Integrate once for \(EI\,\theta\), adding \(C_1\).
  5. Integrate again for \(EI\,y\), adding \(C_2\).
  6. Apply two boundary conditions to evaluate \(C_1\) and \(C_2\).
  7. Substitute the required x to obtain the slope or deflection, then divide by EI in consistent units.

The limitation that motivates the next module: if the loading changes along the span, \(M_x\) has a different expression in each region. Each region then needs its own pair of constants, and continuity of y and θ at every junction must be imposed. A beam with three regions needs six constants and six conditions. Macaulay's method exists to avoid exactly this.

Worked example — cantilever with a point load at the free end

Cantilever AB of length L, fixed at A, carrying W at the free end B. Measure x from A.

Mx = −W(L − x) (hogging, so negative)

EI d²y/dx² = −W(L − x) = −WL + Wx

EI dy/dx = −WLx + Wx²/2 + C₁

At x = 0, θ = 0 (fixed end) → C₁ = 0

EI y = −WLx²/2 + Wx³/6 + C₂

At x = 0, y = 0 → C₂ = 0

At the free end, x = L:

EI θB = −WL² + WL²/2 = −WL²/2 → θB = WL²/(2EI)

EI yB = −WL³/2 + WL³/6 = −WL³/3 → yB = WL³/(3EI) downward

Worked example — simply supported beam with a central point load

Span L, load W at mid-span. By symmetry each reaction is W/2, and the slope is zero at mid-span — use that as the second condition and work only on the left half.

For 0 ≤ x ≤ L/2: Mx = (W/2)x

EI d²y/dx² = Wx/2

EI dy/dx = Wx²/4 + C₁

At x = L/2, θ = 0 (symmetry) → 0 = WL²/16 + C₁ → C₁ = −WL²/16

EI y = Wx³/12 − WL²x/16 + C₂

At x = 0, y = 0 → C₂ = 0

At the support, x = 0: θA = −WL²/(16EI), magnitude WL²/(16EI)

At mid-span, x = L/2: EI y = WL³/96 − WL³/32 = −WL³/48

ymax = WL³/(48EI) downward

Using symmetry saved writing the expression for the right-hand region entirely. Look for it before you start: for any symmetric beam under symmetric loading, θ = 0 at mid-span is free.

Module 10

Macaulay's method — the idea

The problem it solves

A beam with three point loads has four regions, each with its own moment expression. Double integration then produces eight constants, needing eight conditions — two boundary and six continuity. It is arithmetic misery, and it is entirely avoidable.

W. H. Macaulay's observation, published in 1919, was that a single expression can cover the whole beam if terms are allowed to switch themselves off before the load is reached.

The Macaulay bracket

$$ \langle x-a \rangle^n = \begin{cases} (x-a)^n & \text{if } x > a \\[2pt] 0 & \text{if } x \le a \end{cases} $$

The angled bracket is not ordinary multiplication. It is an instruction: if the contents are negative, discard the whole term. A load at 3 m contributes nothing to the moment at 2 m, and the bracket enforces that automatically.

The four rules

  1. Measure x from one end and never change it — conventionally the left end. Every bracket is written as ⟨x − a⟩ with a measured from that same origin.
  2. Take the section beyond the last load, so that the expression written covers the entire beam.
  3. Integrate the bracket as a whole: \(\int \langle x-a \rangle^n dx = \dfrac{\langle x-a \rangle^{n+1}}{n+1}\). Never expand the bracket before integrating — expanding destroys the switching property and is the single most common error.
  4. Apply the boundary conditions with the brackets still intact, deleting any bracket whose contents are negative at that particular x.

Why rule 3 matters

Suppose a term is \(W\langle x-a\rangle^2/2\). Expanding gives \(W(x^2 - 2ax + a^2)/2\), which is a perfectly ordinary polynomial — and it no longer knows to vanish for \(x < a\). Integrate the expanded form and you have silently applied the load to the whole beam, including the part before it exists.

Keep the bracket sealed through both integrations. Open it only at the very last step, when a numerical value of x is substituted — and then only if the contents come out positive.

Module 11

Macaulay's method — worked in full

A simply supported beam of 8 m span carries 40 kN at 2 m from A and 60 kN at 5 m from A. Take EI constant. Find the deflection at mid-span and the maximum deflection.

Step 1 — reactions

ΣMA = 0: RB × 8 = 40 × 2 + 60 × 5 = 80 + 300 = 380

RB = 47.5 kN, RA = 100 − 47.5 = 52.5 kN

Step 2 — one moment expression for the whole beam

Take the section at x measured from A, beyond both loads. Work in kN and m, converting at the end.

EI d²y/dx² = Mx = 52.5x − 40⟨x − 2⟩ − 60⟨x − 5⟩

Integrating once, keeping the brackets sealed:

EI dy/dx = 52.5x²/2 − 40⟨x − 2⟩²/2 − 60⟨x − 5⟩²/2 + C₁

Integrating again:

EI y = 52.5x³/6 − 40⟨x − 2⟩³/6 − 60⟨x − 5⟩³/6 + C₁x + C₂

Step 3 — boundary conditions

At x = 0, y = 0. Both brackets are negative here, so both are discarded:

0 = 0 − (discard) − (discard) + 0 + C₂ → C₂ = 0

At x = 8, y = 0. Now both brackets are positive and both count:

0 = 52.5(512)/6 − 40(6)³/6 − 60(3)³/6 + 8C₁

0 = 4480 − 1440 − 270 + 8C₁

0 = 2770 + 8C₁ → C₁ = −346.25

Note how the two conditions did different amounts of work: at x = 0 every bracket vanished, which is exactly why C₂ is zero for any simply supported beam with the origin at the left support.

Step 4 — the answers

At mid-span, x = 4 m — the first bracket counts, the second does not:

EI y = 52.5(64)/6 − 40(2)³/6 − (discard) − 346.25(4)

EI y = 560 − 53.33 − 1385 = −878.33 kN·m³

y = −878.33 × 10⁹ / EI (converting kN·m³ to N·mm³)

With a 150 × 300 section, EI = 200 000 × 337.5×10⁶ = 6.75 × 10¹³ N·mm²:

y = −878.33 × 10⁹ / 6.75×10¹³ = 13.01 mm downward

The true maximum is where dy/dx = 0. Solving in the region 2 < x < 5:

26.25x² − 20(x − 2)² − 346.25 = 0 → x = 4.05 m

Deflection there = 13.015 mm — only 0.03 % more than at mid-span.

That last observation is worth internalising: for ordinary loading the maximum deflection is so close to mid-span that mid-span is a perfectly good design value. Compute the exact position only when the question demands it.

Unit discipline

Working in kN and m keeps the arithmetic small, but EI y then comes out in kN·m³. To convert to N·mm³ multiply by 10⁹ — a factor of 10³ for kN to N and 10⁶ for m³ to mm³. Getting this conversion wrong is the commonest source of answers that are out by several orders of magnitude, and it is worth writing the conversion explicitly on the answer sheet.

Module 12

Macaulay with a UDL that stops short

The difficulty

A UDL running from \(a\) to \(b\) contributes \(-\dfrac{w}{2}\langle x-a\rangle^2\) once the section passes \(a\). But that term keeps growing for every x beyond \(a\) — including beyond \(b\), where the load has stopped. Left alone, the expression applies the UDL all the way to the right-hand end of the beam.

The remedy

Extend the UDL to the right-hand end of the beam, then add an equal and opposite upward UDL from \(b\) to that end. The two cancel exactly beyond \(b\), and both are now of the form the bracket can handle.

$$ M_x = R_A x - \frac{w}{2}\langle x-a \rangle^2 + \frac{w}{2}\langle x-b \rangle^2 $$

Check it: for \(x < a\) both brackets vanish. For \(a < x < b\) only the first acts, correctly applying the load over the length \((x-a)\). For \(x > b\) the second term subtracts the surplus, leaving exactly \(w(b-a)\) acting at its own centroid. The device is not a trick — it is an exact statement of the same loading.

Worked example

A simply supported beam of 6 m carries a UDL of 20 kN/m over the middle 2 m, from 2 m to 4 m from A. Find the mid-span deflection.

Total load = 20 × 2 = 40 kN at 3 m from A, so by symmetry RA = RB = 20 kN

EI d²y/dx² = 20x − 10⟨x − 2⟩² + 10⟨x − 4⟩²

EI dy/dx = 10x² − (10/3)⟨x − 2⟩³ + (10/3)⟨x − 4⟩³ + C₁

EI y = (10/3)x³ − (5/6)⟨x − 2⟩⁴ + (5/6)⟨x − 4⟩⁴ + C₁x + C₂

At x = 0, y = 0 → C₂ = 0

At x = 6, y = 0: 0 = (10/3)(216) − (5/6)(256) + (5/6)(16) + 6C₁

0 = 720 − 213.33 + 13.33 + 6C₁ → C₁ = −86.67

At mid-span, x = 3 m — the first bracket counts, the second does not:

EI y = (10/3)(27) − (5/6)(1) − 86.67(3) = 90 − 0.833 − 260 = −170.83 kN·m³

With EI = 6.75 × 10¹³ N·mm²: y = −170.83×10⁹ / 6.75×10¹³ = 2.53 mm downward

When the UDL already reaches the end

If the load runs all the way to the right-hand support, no correction term is needed — there is nothing beyond it to cancel. Students sometimes add the cancelling term anyway out of habit, which subtracts a load that was never applied. Add the correction only when the UDL genuinely stops short of the end.

Module 13

Macaulay with an applied couple

The zero-power bracket

An applied couple \(M_0\) at \(x = a\) adds a constant \(M_0\) to the bending moment for every section beyond \(a\), and nothing before it. That is exactly \(M_0\langle x-a\rangle^0\).

$$ \langle x-a\rangle^0 = \begin{cases} 1 & x > a \\ 0 & x \le a \end{cases} \qquad \int \langle x-a\rangle^0 dx = \langle x-a\rangle^1 $$

The zero power looks strange but behaves consistently: it integrates to the first power, then to the second divided by two, exactly as the general rule requires.

Worked example

A simply supported beam of 6 m carries a clockwise couple of 30 kN·m at 2 m from A, with no other load. Find the deflection at the point of application.

ΣFy = 0 gives RA = −RB; ΣMA = 0 gives RB × 6 = 30

RB = +5 kN up, RA = −5 kN, that is 5 kN downward

EI d²y/dx² = −5x + 30⟨x − 2⟩⁰

EI dy/dx = −2.5x² + 30⟨x − 2⟩ + C₁

EI y = −(5/6)x³ + 15⟨x − 2⟩² + C₁x + C₂

At x = 0, y = 0 → C₂ = 0

At x = 6, y = 0: 0 = −(5/6)(216) + 15(16) + 6C₁ = −180 + 240 + 6C₁

C₁ = −10

At x = 2 m, the bracket is exactly zero and contributes nothing:

EI y = −(5/6)(8) + 0 − 10(2) = −6.67 − 20 = −26.67 kN·m³

y = −26.67×10⁹ / 6.75×10¹³ = 0.395 mm downward

Two points of care

  • At exactly x = a the bracket is zero, not one. The condition is \(x > a\), strictly. At the point of application itself the term contributes nothing to the deflection, though the moment diagram jumps there.
  • The couple enters ΣM but never ΣF when finding the reactions — the same rule as Unit 2, Module 13. A beam carrying only a couple has equal and opposite reactions, one of which points downward.

Module 14

The moment-area method

Also called Mohr's theorems. Instead of integrating algebraically, it reads slope and deflection off the area of the bending moment diagram — which you already drew in Unit 2. For cantilevers and symmetric beams it is dramatically faster than Macaulay.

Mohr's first theorem

The change in slope between two points equals the area of the M/EI diagram between them.

$$ \theta_{BA} = \int_A^B \frac{M}{EI}dx $$

Mohr's second theorem

The vertical distance of B from the tangent drawn at A equals the moment of that area about B.

$$ y_{BA} = \int_A^B \frac{M}{EI}x\,dx = A_{M/EI}\cdot \bar{x} $$

Read the second theorem carefully: it gives the distance from the tangent at A, not from the original axis. The two coincide only when the tangent at A is horizontal — which is exactly the case at the fixed end of a cantilever, and at mid-span of a symmetrically loaded beam. That is why the method shines for those two cases and needs care elsewhere.

Worked example — cantilever with a point load at the tip

The tangent at the fixed end A is horizontal, so the deflection of B below the axis equals the moment of the M/EI area about B.

The BMD is a triangle: ordinate WL at A, zero at B

Area of the M/EI diagram = ½ × L × (WL/EI) = WL²/2EI

By the first theorem, θB = WL²/2EI ✓

Centroid of that triangle is at 2L/3 from B (one third from the wide end at A)

By the second theorem, yB = (WL²/2EI) × (2L/3) = WL³/3EI ✓

Two lines of arithmetic against half a page of integration in Module 7 — and no constants of integration at all.

Module 15

Superposition

Because the elastic curve equation is linear in y and in the loads, the deflection caused by several loads acting together is the sum of the deflections each would cause alone.

$$ y_{total} = y_1 + y_2 + y_3 + \dots $$

This turns the standard tables of Modules 8 and 9 into a general-purpose tool: any loading that can be assembled from standard cases can be solved without integrating anything.

Worked example

A simply supported beam of 6 m carries a UDL of 15 kN/m over the whole span together with a point load of 40 kN at mid-span. EI = 6.75 × 10¹³ N·mm². Find the mid-span deflection.

Case 1 — UDL alone: δ₁ = 5wL⁴/384EI

δ₁ = 5 × 15 × 6000⁴ / (384 × 6.75×10¹³) = 3.750 mm

Case 2 — point load alone: δ₂ = WL³/48EI

δ₂ = 40 000 × 6000³ / (48 × 6.75×10¹³) = 2.667 mm

δtotal = 3.750 + 2.667 = 6.417 mm downward

Solving the combined case directly by Macaulay gives 6.4167 mm — identical, as linearity requires.

Where superposition fails

  • Beyond the elastic limit. Once any fibre yields, stress is no longer proportional to strain and the equation is no longer linear.
  • Large deflections. The approximation \(1/R \approx d^2y/dx^2\) was itself a linearisation. Slender members that deflect appreciably need the full expression.
  • When the geometry changes under load. A beam that also carries an axial compression deflects more than the sum of the two effects, because the axial force acts through the lateral displacement — the beam-column problem.
  • Varying EI. Superposition still holds, but the standard table formulas do not, since each assumes constant EI along the span.

The elastic curve

$$EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = M$$

$$EI\frac{dy}{dx} = \int M\,dx + C_1$$

$$EI\,y = \iint M\,dx\,dx + C_1x + C_2$$

Macaulay's bracket

$$\langle x-a\rangle^n = (x-a)^n \text{ if } x>a,\; 0 \text{ otherwise}$$

$$\int \langle x-a\rangle^n dx = \frac{\langle x-a\rangle^{n+1}}{n+1}$$

Mohr's theorems

$$\theta_{AB} = \text{area of the } M/EI \text{ diagram}$$

$$y_{AB} = \text{moment of that area about B}$$

Superposition and checks

$$y_{total} = \sum y_i$$

$$\delta_{permissible} = \frac{\text{span}}{250} \text{ (IS 456 total)}$$

The spine of Unit 4: the bending equation gives curvature M/EI; for small slopes curvature is d²y/dx²; integrating twice gives slope and deflection with two constants; boundary conditions fix the constants. Macaulay makes one expression serve the whole beam, moment-area reads the same integrals off the BMD, and superposition assembles standard cases — three routes to the same two integrations.
