Module 3

Why shear force and bending moment?

Unit 1 dealt with members pulled along their axis, where one number — \(P/A\) — described the whole section. A beam is loaded across its axis, so the internal force varies from point to point along the length. Before you can size a beam you must know two things at every section: how much it is being sliced (shear force) and how much it is being bent (bending moment).

This unit finds the demand; Units 3 and 4 supply the capacity

$$ \sigma_b = \frac{M y}{I} \qquad \tau = \frac{V A \bar y}{I b} $$

Both design formulas begin with \(M\) and \(V\). Everything you do in this unit exists to feed those two symbols with the right number, at the right section.

Six real members, and the beam each one becomes

Every beam problem starts as an object you can walk up to and touch. The engineer's first move is always the same: strip away the material and draw a line with supports and loads on it. Compare the two halves of each figure — that idealisation step carries most of the judgement, and it is the step never marked in an exam yet always deciding whether the answer means anything.

The sleeper is worth a second look

It is the one case where the loads point down at two places and the reaction is spread upward along the whole length — the reverse of every other figure. The result is hogging under the middle and sagging under each rail, which is why a prestressed concrete sleeper is reinforced top and bottom. If you can idealise the sleeper correctly you can idealise anything in this unit.

Where to place steel

In an RCC beam, tension steel goes where the BMD is positive at the bottom and where it is negative at the top. The BMD is literally the drawing the bar bender follows.

Where to add stirrups

Shear is largest near the supports, which is why stirrup spacing is closest there and opens out towards mid-span.

Where to curtail bars

Points of contraflexure tell you where the moment changes sign, and therefore where a bar can be stopped or must be lapped.

The one thing to carry through the unit

A beam does not care what you call positive. It cares that you keep the same rule from the first line of the answer to the last. Fix the convention in Modules 7 and 8, write it at the top of your answer sheet, and never switch it mid-problem.

On a beam carrying only vertical loads the horizontal reaction is zero, so a hinge behaves as a single vertical reaction in the arithmetic. Say so once in your answer rather than ignoring it silently.

Types of beam

Cantilever — fixed at one end, free at the other. Bending moment is hogging throughout and is largest at the fixed end.
Simply supported — a hinge at one end, a roller at the other. Moment is zero at both ends and sagging in between.
Overhanging — the beam extends beyond one or both supports. Produces hogging over the support and sagging in the span, so the BMD crosses zero.
Fixed beam — built in at both ends. Three unknowns per end, so it is statically indeterminate and outside this unit.
Continuous beam — more than two supports. Also indeterminate; solved later by the three-moment equation or moment distribution.
Propped cantilever — fixed at one end with a prop at the other. One redundant reaction.

Static determinacy

A planar beam gives three equations of equilibrium: \(\sum F_x = 0\), \(\sum F_y = 0\), \(\sum M = 0\). Each internal hinge adds one more equation, because the moment there is known to be zero.

$$ D_s = R - (3 + h) $$

\(R\) is the number of reaction components and \(h\) the number of internal hinges. If \(D_s = 0\) the beam is determinate and this unit's methods work. If \(D_s > 0\) it is indeterminate to that degree. If \(D_s < 0\) it is a mechanism and will collapse — check this before you start calculating anything.

Types of load and their resultants

A worked equivalence

A UVL rising from 0 at A to 18 kN/m at B over 6 m has a resultant of ½ × 18 × 6 = 54 kN acting 4 m from A, which is 6/3 = 2 m from B. Students commonly place it at 3 m — the mid-point — which is the rule for a UDL, not a triangle. The centroid of a triangle sits one third of the way from its wide end.

Module 6

Support reactions

The procedure, every time

  1. Draw the free body diagram with all loads and the unknown reactions marked upward.
  2. Replace each distributed load by its resultant at its centroid.
  3. Take moments about one support so that the other reaction drops out: \(\sum M_A = 0\) gives \(R_B\) in one line.
  4. Use \(\sum F_y = 0\) to get the remaining reaction.
  5. Check with \(\sum M_B = 0\). If it does not close, the error is here and not further down — find it now.
$$ R_B = \frac{\sum W_i\,x_i \;+\; \sum M_j}{L_{AB}} \qquad R_A = \sum W_i - R_B $$

Distances \(x_i\) are measured from A, and \(M_j\) are applied couples taken as positive when clockwise.

Worked example

A simply supported beam AB spans 6 m. It carries a UDL of 10 kN/m over the whole span and a point load of 40 kN at 2 m from A.

UDL resultant = 10 × 6 = 60 kN at 3 m from A

ΣMA = 0: RB × 6 = 60 × 3 + 40 × 2 = 180 + 80 = 260

RB = 260 / 6 = 43.33 kN

ΣFy = 0: RA = (60 + 40) − 43.33 = 56.67 kN

Check ΣMB: 56.67 × 6 − 60 × 3 − 40 × 4 = 340 − 180 − 160 = 0 ✓

Three traps in reaction calculations

  • Overhang loads. A load on an overhang still enters ΣF and ΣM. Its moment about the near support is on the opposite side, so it can make the far reaction negative — a genuine downward reaction, and the roller must be able to hold the beam down.
  • Applied couples. A couple has no vertical component, so it appears in ΣM but not in ΣF. If you add it to ΣF the reactions will not sum correctly.
  • Partial UDL. The centroid is at the middle of the loaded length, not the middle of the span.

Module 7

Shear force and its sign convention

Shear force at a section is the algebraic sum of all vertical forces on either side of that section. Cut the beam, discard one side, and the shear force is whatever vertical force the cut face must transmit to keep the remaining piece in equilibrium.

$$ V_x = \sum F_{y,\text{left of }x} $$

Working from the left or from the right gives the same magnitude with opposite algebraic sign from the summation, which is exactly why the convention below is written in terms of one side.

Sign convention used throughout this unit

Positive shear

The resultant of all forces to the left of the section acts upward. Equivalently, the left portion tends to slide up relative to the right portion.

Negative shear

The resultant of forces to the left acts downward. The left portion tends to slide down relative to the right.

This is the left-up-positive rule, the convention used in IS-code practice and in the standard Indian textbooks. Working from the right, the rule inverts: a downward resultant on the right gives positive shear. Both describe the same physical action.

A sign convention is bookkeeping, not physics. Any consistent rule produces the same beam. Errors come from changing the rule halfway through — or from adopting a rule that reads well in one situation and contradicts the moment convention in another. State your rule once, at the top, and hold it.

Two consequences worth memorising: the SFD must close back to zero at the far end of the beam, and the total of all jumps equals the total of all vertical forces. If your SFD does not return to zero, a reaction or a load has been missed.

Module 8

Bending moment and its sign convention

Bending moment at a section is the algebraic sum of the moments, about that section, of all forces on one side of it.

$$ M_x = \sum \big( F_{\text{left}} \times \text{distance to the section} \big) $$

Sagging and hogging

Sagging — positive

The beam bends into a smile. Tension at the bottom fibre, compression at the top. The moment of the left-hand forces about the section is clockwise.

Hogging — negative

The beam bends into a frown. Tension at the top fibre, compression at the bottom. Standard over the supports of a continuous beam and throughout a cantilever.

Why this matters more than the shear convention: the sign of the moment tells the detailer which face of the beam goes into tension, and steel must be placed on the tension face. Get the sign wrong and the reinforcement ends up on the wrong side of the beam.

Behaviour of the bending moment diagram

Along the beamBMD does thisWhy
No loadStraight sloping linedM/dx = V, a constant
Under a UDLParabola, second degreeV varies linearly, so M is quadratic
Under a UVLCubic curveOne further integration
At a point loadSharp kink, no jumpThe slope changes abruptly because V jumps
At an applied couple M₀Sudden jump of M₀A concentrated moment must appear somewhere
At a free end or a simple end supportZeroNothing beyond it to produce a moment
The degree rule. Each step from load to shear to moment raises the degree of the curve by one. Point load → SFD constant → BMD linear. UDL → SFD linear → BMD parabolic. UVL → SFD parabolic → BMD cubic. This single rule catches most sketching errors before you draw anything.

Module 9

Relations between load, shear force and bending moment

Derivation from an element

Take an element of length \(dx\) carrying a UDL of intensity \(w\). Let the shear and moment on the left face be \(V\) and \(M\), and on the right face \(V + dV\) and \(M + dM\).

  1. Vertical equilibrium: \(V - w\,dx - (V + dV) = 0\)
  2. Moment equilibrium about the right face, dropping the second-order term \(w\,dx^2/2\): \(M + V\,dx - (M + dM) = 0\)
$$ \frac{dV}{dx} = -w $$
$$ \frac{dM}{dx} = V $$
$$ \frac{d^2M}{dx^2} = -w $$

What each relation buys you

Slope of the SFD equals minus the load intensity. A heavier UDL gives a steeper SFD. Where there is no load the SFD is flat.
Slope of the BMD equals the shear force. So the bending moment is maximum where the shear force is zero or changes sign. This is the single most useful result in the unit.
Change in shear equals the area of the load diagram. \(V_2 - V_1 = -\int w\,dx\).
Change in moment equals the area of the SFD. \(M_2 - M_1 = \int V\,dx\). This lets you build the BMD by adding areas off the SFD instead of taking moments at every section — much faster in an exam.

Area method — worked

Simply supported beam, 8 m span, UDL 12 kN/m. Reactions are 48 kN each.

V at A (just right) = +48 kN, falling at 12 kN per metre

V = 0 at x = 48/12 = 4 m — the mid-point, so the maximum moment is there

Area of the SFD from A to 4 m = ½ × 4 × 48 = 96

M at 4 m = M at A + area = 0 + 96 = 96 kN·m

Cross-check with the standard result wL²/8 = 12 × 64 / 8 = 96 kN·m ✓

Notice that no section-by-section moment equation was written. For any beam whose SFD you can draw, the BMD follows by accumulating areas from a known zero.

Module 12

Overhanging beams and contraflexure

An overhang produces hogging over the support and sagging within the span. Somewhere between them the bending moment passes through zero: that section is a point of contraflexure, also called a point of inflexion.

What it is
A section where M = 0 and the curvature changes sign.
How to find it
Write M as a function of x for the relevant region and set it to zero. Only roots inside that region count.
Why it matters
The tension face swaps from bottom to top there, so it fixes where main bars are curtailed or lapped.

Do not confuse it with the point of zero shear. Zero shear locates the maximum moment; zero moment locates contraflexure. They are different sections and both may be asked in the same question.

Worked example — the classic

A beam ABC is 6 m long, supported at A (x = 0) and B (x = 4 m), with a 2 m overhang to C. It carries a UDL of 10 kN/m over the entire 6 m.

Total load = 10 × 6 = 60 kN acting at 3 m from A

ΣMA = 0: RB × 4 = 60 × 3 = 180 → RB = 45 kN

ΣFy = 0: RA = 60 − 45 = 15 kN

V(x) = 15 − 10x for 0 < x < 4. Zero shear at x = 15/10 = 1.5 m

M(1.5) = 15 × 1.5 − 10 × 1.5 × 0.75 = 22.5 − 11.25 = +11.25 kN·m (maximum sagging)

M at B = 15 × 4 − 10 × 4 × 2 = 60 − 80 = −20 kN·m (maximum hogging)

Contraflexure: 15x − 5x² = 0 → x(15 − 5x) = 0 → x = 3 m from A

Check M at C: 15 × 6 + 45 × 2 − 10 × 6 × 3 = 90 + 90 − 180 = 0 ✓

Note that the numerically largest moment, 20 kN·m, is the hogging one over the support — not the sagging one in the span. On overhanging beams the support section usually governs the design, and students who only look at mid-span miss it.

Rule of thumb

Hogging over a support balances sagging in the span. Lengthen the overhang and the hogging grows while the span sagging shrinks. For a beam of total length L with two equal overhangs under a UDL, the two are equal when each overhang is a = 0.207 L: hogging wa²/2 equals sagging w(L−2a)²/8 − wa²/2. That is the most economical proportion, and it is why canopy slabs and balanced-cantilever girders are often set out this way.

Module 13

Beams carrying applied couples

A couple applied at a section — from an eccentric bracket, a cantilevered sign, or a column moment — has no vertical component. It changes the moment diagram without touching the shear diagram.

Effect on the SFD
None. The shear force passes straight through the section unchanged, because ΣFy is unaffected.
Effect on the BMD
A sudden vertical jump equal to the magnitude of the couple, in the direction the couple acts.

The couple does enter the reaction calculation: it appears in ΣM but not in ΣF. On a simply supported beam carrying a couple alone, the two reactions form an equal and opposite pair — one up, one down.

Worked example

A simply supported beam AB of 6 m carries a clockwise couple of 30 kN·m at 3 m from A. There are no other loads.

ΣFy = 0: RA + RB = 0, so the reactions are equal and opposite

ΣMA = 0: 30 − RB × 6 = 0 → RB = +5 kN (up)

RA = −5 kN, that is 5 kN acting downward — the support must hold the beam down

Shear is constant at −5 kN over the whole span. The SFD is a single horizontal line.

M just left of 3 m = −5 × 3 = −15 kN·m

M just right of 3 m = −15 + 30 = +15 kN·m — a jump of exactly 30

M at B = −5 × 6 + 30 = 0 ✓

Where students lose marks

  • Adding the couple to ΣFy. It has no vertical force, so the reactions come out wrong from the first line.
  • Drawing a jump in the SFD at the couple. The SFD is untouched.
  • Jumping the BMD in the wrong direction. Evaluate M just left and just right of the section rather than guessing.
  • Forgetting that a downward reaction is a legitimate answer. If the arithmetic gives a negative reaction, redraw the arrow and carry on.

Module 14

Beams with an internal hinge

An internal hinge in the span transmits shear but cannot transmit moment. That gives one extra equation, \(M = 0\) at the hinge, which is what makes an otherwise indeterminate beam solvable by statics alone.

$$ M_{\text{hinge}} = 0 $$

Structures built this way are called compound or Gerber beams. Bridge girders often use them so that the deck can be built in determinate segments and still accommodate settlement.

Solution method

  1. Identify which part of the beam is supported and which part is suspended — the suspended part cannot stand without the other.
  2. Cut at the hinge and analyse the suspended part first. It is a simple beam or a cantilever in its own right.
  3. The vertical force at the hinge, found from that analysis, is applied as a point load on the supported part, acting downward.
  4. Analyse the supported part with that extra load and find its reactions.
  5. Draw one continuous SFD and BMD across the whole beam. The BMD must pass through zero at the hinge — that is the built-in check.

Worked example

Beam ABCD: A is a fixed support at x = 0. There is an internal hinge at C, x = 6 m. D is a roller at x = 9 m. A UDL of 8 kN/m runs over the whole 9 m.

Suspended part CD, 3 m long with 8 kN/m, hinged at C and on a roller at D:

Total load = 8 × 3 = 24 kN. It behaves as a simply supported span of 3 m.

RD = 24/2 = 12 kN, and the vertical force at the hinge VC = 12 kN

Supported part AC, a 6 m cantilever fixed at A, with 8 kN/m plus 12 kN transferred down at C:

RA = 8 × 6 + 12 = 48 + 12 = 60 kN

MA = −(8 × 6 × 3) − (12 × 6) = −144 − 72 = −216 kN·m (hogging)

Check at C: M = −216 + 60 × 6 − 8 × 6 × 3 = −216 + 360 − 144 = 0 ✓ — the hinge condition is satisfied

Maximum sagging in CD = 8 × 3² / 8 = 9 kN·m at 1.5 m from C

The check at the hinge is not optional. If M does not come out as zero there, the transferred hinge force is wrong and everything downstream is wrong with it.

Module 15

Locating the maximum bending moment

Since \(dM/dx = V\), the bending moment is stationary where the shear force is zero. Design is governed by the largest magnitude of moment anywhere on the beam, whether it is sagging or hogging.

$$ M \text{ is maximum where } V = 0 \text{ or where } V \text{ changes sign} $$

Where to look, in order

  1. Where the SFD crosses zero within a distributed load. Solve V(x) = 0 for x, then evaluate M(x). This is the usual case.
  2. At every point load. Under a point load the SFD jumps, so it may cross zero without ever equalling zero. The maximum then sits exactly at the load, and you find it by evaluating M there rather than by solving an equation.
  3. At every support of an overhanging or fixed beam. Hogging peaks there and frequently exceeds the span sagging.
  4. At every applied couple. The BMD jumps, so check the value on both sides.
The trap. If solving V(x) = 0 gives a value of x that falls outside the region where that expression is valid, the root is spurious. Discard it and check the section where the shear actually changes sign — almost always a point load. This is the single most common error in university answer scripts on this topic.

Worked example — the trap in action

Simply supported beam of 6 m with a UDL of 10 kN/m over the whole span and a point load of 40 kN at 2 m from A. From Module 6, RA = 56.67 kN and RB = 43.33 kN.

Region 0 < x < 2: V = 56.67 − 10x. Setting V = 0 gives x = 5.67 m, which is outside this region — reject it.

Region 2 < x < 6: V = 56.67 − 40 − 10x = 16.67 − 10x. Setting V = 0 gives x = 1.67 m, again outside the region — reject it.

Neither region contains a zero, so the shear must change sign at the point load.

V just left of 2 m = 56.67 − 20 = +36.67 kN

V just right of 2 m = 36.67 − 40 = −3.33 kN → sign change confirmed at x = 2 m

Mmax = 56.67 × 2 − 10 × 2 × 1 = 113.33 − 20 = 93.33 kN·m at 2 m from A

What to notice
