Module 3

Why study stress?

A structure does not fail because the load is "large". It fails because the load, divided by the area carrying it, exceeds what the material can bear. That ratio is stress. Every design code — IS 800, IS 456, IS 883 — is at bottom a set of limits on stress.

The whole subject in one line

$$ \text{Safe design} \iff \sigma_{\text{induced}} \le \sigma_{\text{permissible}} $$

Where it shows up

Bridge members

Truss ties carry pure tension; a single under-sized gusset plate can unzip the whole span.

Building cracks

Concrete is weak in tension. Cracks map out exactly where tensile stress ran ahead of capacity.

Machine shafts

Torsional shear stress peaks at the surface, so shafts fail from the outside in.

Aircraft skin

Pressurisation cycles a fuselage like a balloon. Stress concentration at a window corner started the Comet failures.

Railway track

Welded rail cannot expand. Summer heat converts free expansion into compressive stress and buckling.

Pressure vessels

Hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress, which is why cylinders split along their length.

Two case studies worth knowing

Hyatt Regency walkway, 1981

A change from a single continuous hanger rod to two shorter rods doubled the load on the upper box beam connection. The bearing area at the nut stayed the same while the force doubled — the stress doubled and the connection tore through. A drafting-level change, not a loading change.

Continuous welded rail buckling

Rail restrained end to end develops \(\sigma = \alpha\,\Delta T\,E\). For steel with \(\Delta T = 40^\circ\)C that is about 96 MPa of compression with no external load at all. Module 14 derives this.

Module 4

Internal and external forces

External force

Applied from outside the body — dead load, live load, wind, reactions at supports. Visible on the free body diagram.

Internal resisting force

Developed inside the material to hold the body together. Revealed only when you cut a section. Equal and opposite to the external force on that side of the cut.

Method of sections, in one sentence: cut the member, throw away one side, and the internal force at the cut must equal whatever is needed to keep the remaining piece in equilibrium — \(\sum F = 0\).

Sign convention used throughout this unit

Tension is positive

The force pulls away from the cut face. The member gets longer, \(\delta L\) positive.

Compression is negative

The force pushes into the cut face. The member shortens, \(\delta L\) negative.

A sign convention is a bookkeeping choice, not a law of nature. Any consistent convention gives the same physical answer. What causes errors is switching convention mid-problem — so fix one at the start and write it on your answer sheet.

Types of load

Point

Concentrated at one location, units of N or kN.

UDL

Spread evenly along a length, units of N/m or kN/m.

Axial

Acts along the centroidal axis — the only case where \(\sigma = P/A\) is uniform.

Eccentric

Parallel to the axis but offset, so it adds bending to the direct stress.

Shear

Transverse, tending to slide one face past the adjacent face.

Torsion

A twisting couple about the longitudinal axis.

Bending

A couple in the plane of the member, producing tension on one face and compression on the other.

Impact

Applied suddenly; the instantaneous stress can be twice the static value.

Module 5

Stress — formula and units

Definition

Stress is the internal resisting force developed per unit of cross-sectional area.

Derivation from first principles

  1. Take a prismatic bar carrying an axial pull \(P\).
  2. Cut it by a plane normal to the axis; area of the cut face is \(A\).
  3. For the free body to remain in equilibrium, the cut face must transmit a resultant \(R = P\).
  4. Assume the resistance is shared uniformly over the face — true for an axial load applied at the centroid, far from the ends (Saint-Venant's principle). Then intensity \(= R/A\).
$$ \sigma \;=\; \lim_{\Delta A \to 0}\frac{\Delta R}{\Delta A} \;=\; \frac{P}{A} $$
\(\sigma\) — normal stress, N/mm² = MPa
\(P\) — axial force, N
\(A\) — area normal to \(P\), mm²

Single shear versus double shear

Single shear — lap joint

One failure plane through the bolt.

$$ \tau = \frac{P}{\tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2} $$

Double shear — butt joint

Two failure planes, so each carries half the load.

$$ \tau = \frac{P}{2 \times \tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2} $$

Forgetting the factor of 2 in double shear is the most common bolt-design error in papers. Count the planes on the sketch before you write the formula.

Module 7

Strain and deformation

Strain is the deformation per unit original dimension. It is a ratio, so it has no unit — never write MPa or mm against a strain value.

Linear (longitudinal) strain

$$ \varepsilon = \frac{\delta L}{L} $$

Along the direction of the load.

Lateral strain

$$ \varepsilon_{lat} = \frac{\delta d}{d} = -\mu\,\varepsilon $$

Across the load. Opposite in sign to linear strain.

Volumetric strain

$$ \varepsilon_v = \frac{\delta V}{V} = \varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y + \varepsilon_z $$

For a bar under one-directional stress this reduces to \(\varepsilon_v = \varepsilon(1-2\mu)\).

Shear strain

$$ \phi = \frac{\delta_s}{h} \approx \tan\phi $$

Angular distortion in radians, measured as sideways movement over height.

Thermal strain

$$ \varepsilon_t = \alpha\,\Delta T $$

Occurs with no load at all. If the member is free, this strain produces deformation but no stress. Stress appears only when expansion is restrained — Module 14.

Deformation of an axially loaded bar

$$ \delta L = \frac{PL}{AE} $$

Derived by substituting \(\sigma = P/A\) and \(\varepsilon = \delta L/L\) into \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon\). The group \(AE\) is the axial rigidity of the member — the axial equivalent of stiffness.

Stepped bar: stress changes at each change of area, so add the segments:

$$ \delta L_{total} = \sum_{i} \frac{P_i L_i}{A_i E_i} $$

Strain magnitudes — build intuition

Steel at working stress strains by roughly 0.0006. That is 0.6 mm in a metre. Structural strains are invisible to the eye, which is exactly why they must be calculated.

Module 8

Hooke's law

"Within the elastic limit, stress is directly proportional to strain."

$$ \sigma \propto \varepsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma = E\,\varepsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} $$

The constant of proportionality \(E\) is Young's modulus. Because strain is dimensionless, \(E\) carries the units of stress. It measures stiffness, not strength — a stiff material resists deformation; a strong material resists failure. Steel and high-strength steel have nearly the same \(E\) but very different yield stress.

Steel
200 GPa
Aluminium
70 GPa
Concrete M25

Limitations — where the law stops working

  • Valid only up to the proportional limit, not up to fracture.
  • Does not apply to materials with no straight initial portion — cast iron, concrete in compression, rubber.
  • Assumes the material is homogeneous, isotropic and continuous. Timber and FRP are not isotropic.
  • Ignores time effects. Under sustained load, concrete creeps and steel at high temperature relaxes; strain keeps growing at constant stress.
  • Applies to small strains. Beyond that, cross-sectional area changes appreciably and engineering stress is no longer the real stress.

Module 10

Curves for other materials

Toggle materials to compare. Curves are schematic and drawn to a common strain axis.

Cast iron

No straight portion, no yield point, fractures at about 1 % strain. Brittle — strong in compression, weak in tension, fails without warning. Hooke's law is used only as an approximation over a small range.

TOR / HYSD steel (Fe415, Fe500)

Cold-worked, so there is no distinct yield point. The design yield is taken as the 0.2 % proof stress — draw a line parallel to the elastic slope from ε = 0.002 and take where it cuts the curve.

Aluminium

Smooth curve, no sharp yield, E ≈ 70 GPa — one third of steel, so an aluminium member deflects three times as much for the same stress. Proof stress is again used.

Rubber

Non-linear from the start and elastic to enormous strains (300 % or more). Elastic does not mean linear — rubber returns to shape but never obeys Hooke's law.

Module 11

Elastic constants

Young's modulus, E

$$ E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} $$

Direct stress over direct strain. Steel: 200 GPa.

Modulus of rigidity, G (or C, N)

$$ G = \frac{\tau}{\phi} $$

Shear stress over shear strain. Steel: about 80 GPa.

Bulk modulus, K

$$ K = \frac{\sigma_v}{\varepsilon_v} $$

Hydrostatic stress over volumetric strain. Steel: about 167 GPa.

Poisson's ratio, μ (or 1/m)

$$ \mu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{lat}}{\varepsilon_{long}} $$

Dimensionless, between 0 and 0.5. Steel 0.3, concrete 0.15, cork ≈ 0.

Relations between the constants

$$ E = 2G(1+\mu) $$
$$ E = 3K(1-2\mu) $$
$$ E = \frac{9KG}{3K+G} $$

Only two of the four are independent for an isotropic material. Give any two and the other two follow.

Memory aids

  • "2G before 3K" — the 2 goes with G, the 3 goes with K, in alphabetical order G then K.
  • Signs follow the physics — shear does not change volume, so \(\mu\) is added: \((1+\mu)\). Bulk compression is resisted by lateral effects, so \(\mu\) is subtracted: \((1-2\mu)\).
  • Check \(\mu = 0.5\) — then \(E = 3K(0) = 0\), meaning \(K \to \infty\): an incompressible material. That is the physical upper bound on \(\mu\).
  • Order of size for steel: \(K > E > G\) — 167 > 200? No: for μ = 0.3, \(E = 200\), \(K = 167\), \(G = 77\). So \(E > K > G\). Check your answer against this ranking.

Module 12

The Poisson effect

Pull a bar and it gets longer — and thinner. The lateral contraction accompanying axial extension is the Poisson effect, and the ratio of the two strains is a material constant.

$$ \varepsilon_{lat} = -\mu\,\varepsilon_{long} \qquad \varepsilon_v = \varepsilon(1-2\mu) $$

Why μ cannot exceed 0.5

From \(\varepsilon_v = \varepsilon(1-2\mu)\): at \(\mu = 0.5\) the volumetric strain is zero, so the material deforms at constant volume — rubber and water behave close to this. If \(\mu\) were greater than 0.5, a bar under tension would have to lose volume, which no ordinary material does. The lower bound for isotropic materials is \(-1\), and a few engineered auxetic foams do have negative μ: stretch them and they get fatter.

Module 13

Composite (compound) bars

Two or more materials rigidly connected and sharing one load — a steel rod inside a copper tube, an RCC column, a bimetallic strip. The load division is not known from statics alone, so the problem is statically indeterminate. You need a second equation, and it comes from geometry.

1. Equilibrium

$$ P = P_1 + P_2 = \sigma_1 A_1 + \sigma_2 A_2 $$

2. Compatibility

$$ \delta_1 = \delta_2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\sigma_1 L}{E_1} = \frac{\sigma_2 L}{E_2} $$

Equal length means equal strain, so with the same \(L\):

$$ \frac{\sigma_1}{E_1} = \frac{\sigma_2}{E_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma_1 = \sigma_2\,\frac{E_1}{E_2} = m\,\sigma_2 $$

Modular ratio \(m = E_1/E_2\). The stiffer material attracts stress in proportion to its modulus — this is the single idea the whole topic rests on.

Worked example

A steel rod of 1000 mm² is encased in a copper tube of 2000 mm². The composite column, 500 mm long, carries 250 kN. Take \(E_s = 200\) GPa, \(E_c = 100\) GPa.

Strain is common: ε = P / (AsEs + AcEc)

ε = 250 000 / (1000×200 000 + 2000×100 000) = 250 000 / 400 000 000 = 6.25×10⁻⁴

σs = Eε = 200 000 × 6.25×10⁻⁴ = 125 MPa

σc = 100 000 × 6.25×10⁻⁴ = 62.5 MPa

Ps = 125 × 1000 = 125 kN    Pc = 62.5 × 2000 = 125 kN   (sum = 250 kN ✓)

δ = εL = 6.25×10⁻⁴ × 500 = 0.3125 mm

Note the result: the copper has twice the area but half the modulus, so the two carry exactly the same force. Area and stiffness both matter, and the product \(AE\) is what does the sharing.

Module 14

Temperature stress

Free expansion — strain, no stress

$$ \delta = \alpha\,\Delta T\,L $$

The bar lengthens freely. Nothing resists it, so no internal force develops.

Fully restrained — stress, no strain

$$ \sigma = \alpha\,\Delta T\,E $$

Supports prevent the expansion. The bar is effectively pushed back to its original length, and \(L\) cancels out — the stress does not depend on the length.

Partial restraint (a gap \(\Delta\) is provided): only the expansion beyond the gap is resisted.

$$ \sigma = \frac{E\,(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L - \Delta)}{L} $$

If \(\alpha \Delta T L \le \Delta\) the bar never touches the far support and the stress is zero. Always check this first.

Composite bar under temperature change

When two bonded materials with different α are heated, the one that wants to expand more is held back (goes into compression) and the other is dragged along (goes into tension). Since no external load acts:

$$ \sigma_1 A_1 = \sigma_2 A_2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{\sigma_1}{E_1} + \frac{\sigma_2}{E_2} = (\alpha_1 - \alpha_2)\,\Delta T $$

Take \(\alpha_1 > \alpha_2\). The first equation is equilibrium of the internal pull-and-push pair; the second says the strain difference the two would have had is exactly taken up by the elastic strains. This is the bimetallic strip, and it is why a thermostat bends.

Module 15

Factor of safety

No structure is designed at the stress that breaks it. A margin covers variation in material, errors in load estimation, workmanship, corrosion and the consequences of failure.

Brittle materials

$$ \text{FOS} = \frac{\sigma_{ultimate}}{\sigma_{working}} $$

Ductile materials

$$ \text{FOS} = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{working}} $$

For ductile materials, yield — not fracture — is the limit of usefulness, because a permanently bent member has already failed to do its job.

TermMeaning
Working / actual stressWhat the member really carries under service load
Permissible / allowable stressThe ceiling set by the code = limiting stress ÷ FOS
Limiting stressYield for ductile, ultimate for brittle

Typical values: steel in tension 1.5–2.0, concrete 3.0, timber 4.0–6.0, and higher again where failure endangers life or the loading is poorly known.

Design a member

Working stress versus limit state

The FOS above belongs to working stress design: one factor applied to the material. Modern codes including IS 456 and IS 800 use limit state design, applying a partial safety factor to the material (γm) and separate factors to each load (γf) — for example 1.5 on dead and live load, 1.15 on steel, 1.5 on concrete. The philosophy is the same margin, distributed where the uncertainty actually lies.
